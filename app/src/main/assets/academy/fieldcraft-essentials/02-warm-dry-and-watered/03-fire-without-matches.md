Fire gives warmth, dry clothes, hot food, safe water and a signal. Learn it before you need it.

## The three parts
**Tinder** (catches a spark: dry grass, birch bark, cattail fluff, fine wood shavings, cotton balls with petroleum jelly), **kindling** (pencil-lead to thumb thickness), and **fuel** (wrist-thick and bigger). **Gather everything before you strike a spark.** Gather three times as much as you think you need.

## With a lighter or matches
Build a small teepee of kindling over a nest of tinder, leave a gap on the windward side, light the tinder, and add kindling a little at a time.

## With a ferrocerium rod
Scrape the rod hard and fast with a knife spine, pointing sparks into a dry tinder nest. Practise with a dry nest first.

## With nothing
- **Magnifying lens or clear water in a bag** in bright sun, aimed at fine dark tinder.
- **Bow drill** (friction): a dry soft wood spindle and board, a bow with cord, and a bearing block. It takes days of practice. Learn it before you need it.

## Wet weather
- Split wood. The inside is dry.
- Use standing dead twigs from the lower branches of evergreens, not ground wood.
- Build on a platform of sticks or bark so the ground does not smother the fire.

## Safety
Clear the ground to bare dirt for six feet. Keep water or dirt close. Never leave a fire alone. Drown it, stir it, feel it with the back of your hand, and drown it again.

## Do it
- [ ] Light a fire with a ferro rod and a tinder nest you gathered yourself.
- [ ] Light a fire in drizzle or after a rain, using split wood.

## Check yourself

? What do you do before you strike the first spark?
- Look for a signal
- Boil water
- Walk away
+ Gather tinder, kindling and fuel
> A spark dies fast if you cannot feed it.

? Where is wood still dry in the rain?
- On the ground
+ Inside split logs and on dead lower branches of standing trees
- In puddles
- In wet leaves
> Split the wood and use standing deadfall.

? How do you put a campfire out?
- Cover with leaves
- Kick dirt on top once
- Leave it to burn out
+ Drown it, stir it and feel for heat
> Embers can stay hot for hours.
