A shelter has one job: keep the wind, rain and ground cold away from your body. Small is warm. A shelter just big enough to lie in holds your heat; a big one only holds air.

## Pick the spot
- **Near** water, firewood and your signal spot, but **not** at the water's edge where cold air pools or floods rise.
- **Away from** dead trees and heavy limbs ("widow makers"), dry creek beds, and lone tall trees in storms.
- Flat, well drained, and out of the wind.

## A debris hut (works anywhere trees fall)
1. Lean a long, strong ridge pole against a stump or fork so it sits about waist high at the head and lower at the feet.
2. Lean branches along both sides like ribs.
3. Cover the ribs with brush, then pile **at least two feet** of dry leaves, pine needles or grass on top.
4. Stuff the inside with dry leaves for a bed at least a foot thick.
5. Block the door with a pack or a bundle of leaves once you are in.

## A tarp or trash bag shelter
Tie a line between two trees at chest height, drape the sheet over it, and peg or weight the edges. Face the low end into the wind.

## Common mistakes
Building too big, skipping the bed (the ground steals more heat than the air), and building in a hollow where cold air settles.

## Do it
- [ ] Build a debris hut or tarp shelter and spend a night in it, close to home.
- [ ] Check that your bed is at least a foot of dry material.

## Check yourself

? How big should an emergency shelter be?
- As big as a room
+ Just big enough to lie in
- As tall as you can reach
- Big enough for a fire inside
> A small space is warmed by your own body.

? Which matters most for warmth?
- A pretty shape
- A tall roof
- Being close to the road
+ Insulation under you and over you
> Ground contact steals heat quickly.

? Where should you NOT build?
- On flat, drained ground
- Out of the wind
+ Under dead limbs or in a dry creek bed
- Near firewood
> Falling limbs and flash floods are real killers.
