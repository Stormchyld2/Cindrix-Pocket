## Purpose, and the one rule
This procedure keeps the dead from harming the living, mainly through drinking water. It covers four kinds of remains, each with its own steps: **people, livestock, pets, and wildlife**. Dead bodies from an injury, drowning or disaster are not a big disease risk by themselves. The real risks are contaminated water, the person handling the body, and bodies that were sick with something catching.
- **The one rule: never choose a burial place from a distance alone.** A distance from a well is a minimum, not a guarantee. Check the soil, how deep the groundwater is, where water drains, and what the law says, first.
- When in doubt, hold the remains cold and wait. A short delay costs little. A poisoned well can end a stay.
- Treat all drinking water anyway, always (filter, then boil or disinfect), whatever you buried.

## Three stages: do the hard part now
This procedure is written to work in three stages, because the help you can call on will shrink.
- **Now (services work):** do all the checking you can that needs outside help or the internet: print soil, geology and karst maps, ask the county and neighbours, choose and record sites, learn the law, buy the kit, get vaccinations such as tetanus up to date. This is the cheapest time to get it right.
- **When services thin out (weeks to months):** no renderer or landfill pick-up, slow or no officials. Use the methods you can run yourselves, and your own records.
- **When they are gone (a year or more):** you are the cemetery, the vet and the health department. Everything below that says "ask" or "call" has a version that works without anyone to ask. The rules then are the ones you keep yourselves: protect the water, keep records, keep the dignity of the dead, and hold the group to it.
Nothing here assumes the law will be enforced or that anyone will check your work. That makes the checking more important, not less.

## Decide who decides
Name one person to lead this work and a second to check it, before it is needed. For a death in the family or group, the family decides the arrangements, and the leader makes sure the site and the safety steps are done. Write the names down.

- No one works alone. Two people at every step.
- Nobody handles remains who has an open wound they cannot cover, or who is very upset. Ask a steady person instead.
- Keep the children out of the work. Tell them honestly and gently what is happening.

## Get ready now: what to have before you need it
Keep a small kit, together and labelled.
- Gloves (disposable, and heavy rubber ones for digging and lifting), waterproof apron or coveralls, boots, eye protection or a face shield, a mask if you like.
- Heavy plastic sheeting, sturdy body bags or a clean sheet and rope, labels and a marker that does not run.
- Soap, a jug of treated water for hand washing, a bucket, towels, a spare change of clothes.
- Shovels, a pick, a probe bar or long steel rod, a tape measure, a notebook and pencil, a camera if you have one.
- Printed maps of your land: the soil map (Web Soil Survey), the geology or karst map, and your own site sketch. Print them while the internet works.

## Choose your sites now, before you need them
Pick, check and record, in advance, a **family or group burial ground**, a **compost pad for animals**, and a **game-scraps pit**. Choosing in calm weather, with the maps, is far safer than choosing at a graveside.
- Put them on your sketch, down-slope and away from your water, and keep them out of the way of your gardens and where you will build or dig a well.
- Use one burial ground for the group, not scattered graves: it is easier to protect, map and keep respectful.
- Use the site checks below on each one, and write the Site Check sheet now. If a site fails, choose another now.
- Record the sites in the group's book and in two places, and tell everyone who might have to use them.

## Site assessment, step 1: paper first

button: Open Maps
Before you pick a place, learn what your ground is. Do this now, not in a crisis, for each area you might use.
- **Soil:** the USDA soil survey for your parcel shows soil type, depth to bedrock, depth to the seasonal high water table, flooding, and how fast water moves through. Print it.
- **Geology:** is your land on limestone or dolomite? Two thirds of Tennessee is. If so, treat the whole property as karst until you have shown otherwise (see the next step).
- **Water:** mark every well, spring, creek, pond, wet-weather ditch, drain tile and low spot on your sketch, including those on neighbours' land and downhill.
- **Rules:** check your county's rules (zoning, health department) and your state's. Write down who you asked and what they said.

## Site assessment, step 2: karst check
Karst is limestone ground with sinkholes, caves, springs and underground streams. Water, and anything in it, can move quickly and far underground, in ways a measured distance on the surface does not predict. Soil over limestone can be thin, and runoff can drop straight into sinkholes and cracks.
- **Warning signs:** sinkholes or closed dips with no outlet, streams that disappear, springs or caves, bare limestone, very thin soil, cedar-and-rock "glades", and a history of sinkholes in the area.
- **Never** bury anything in, beside or upslope of a sinkhole, cave opening, losing stream or spring. Never dump into, or fill in, a sinkhole.
- **In karst:** do not assume a burial site is safe because it is far from your well. A neighbour's well or a spring far away may be fed from the same underground water.
- **Ask now, while you can:** the county Extension office, your NRCS field office and the state geology and water agency know where the karst is. Get their maps and answers on paper. **Ask the oldest local people too**: where the ground has opened, where creeks vanish, where springs run, where a pond drained overnight. In a long grid-down this local knowledge, plus your own eyes, is all you will have.
- If you cannot show deep, tight soil over solid ground, **do not bury there**. Find a better site (see "Doing the checks yourself") or use another method (see the alternatives).

## Doing the checks yourself (no experts, no maps)
You can still tell a lot with a shovel, a rod, time and rain.
- **Walk the whole property after a heavy rain** and watch where water goes: into the ground at a low spot, into a hole, along a ditch. Water that vanishes into a dip or crack is a warning. Flag it and stay away.
- **Look for the signs:** closed dips with no outlet, bare limestone, cedar-and-rock ground, springs, caves, creeks that disappear, ground that has sunk.
- **Prefer high, flat or gently sloping ground with deep soil:** dig a test hole to 6 ft (about 2 m) at several spots. Deep, even clay-loam with no rock, gravel, cracks, roots-only-in-a-seam or water is the best you will find. Rock, gravel or water in the hole means move.
- **Dig the test hole in the wet season** (or after long rain) and look again a day later: does water seep in? Then it is too wet. Do not trust a dry-season hole.
- **Mark every spring, seep and well** and bury only down-slope of them, and as far from all of them as you can, much further than the minimum.
- **Treat the doubt as a no.** If you cannot show a site is safe, it is not. Pick the other method instead of a risky burial.
- **Your water treatment is your last line of defence.** Whatever you do, filter and boil or disinfect every drop you drink, from every source, every time, for as long as the group lives there.

## Site assessment, step 3: field check
Walk the site and probe it. Record each answer on the Site Check sheet (see Records).
- Dig a test hole or use a probe to the depth you would bury. Note soil type (clay, silt, sand, gravel), moisture, colour and depth to rock or water. Gravel, sand, cracked or very shallow soil is a **no**.
- Dig it in the **wettest season** if you can, or look for signs of the high water table: mottled or grey soil, springs, standing water, wetland plants.
- Check slope and flow: where does rain run after a storm? Water must run away from the site, never into it.
- Check floods: do not use a floodplain, a flood path or a spot that ponds.
- Check for pipes, tile drains, septic fields, buried lines and old graves before digging. Call the utility or ask the neighbours.
- If any answer is doubtful, choose another site or another method.

## Groundwater protection: what the numbers are, and what they are not
Guidance from emergency-health and farm agencies sets minimums. They assume ordinary ground and do not make karst safe.
- **People (WHO, PAHO, ICRC and IFRC field manual, 2nd edition, 2016):** individual graves 1.5 to 3 m (5 to 10 ft) deep; at least 30 m (100 ft) from springs and streams and 200 m (650 ft) from any well or other source of drinking water (its planning annex says 250 m for wells and 10 m from field drains); at least 0.9 to 1.2 m (3 to 4 ft) of soil over the body; and for fewer than five graves at least 1.2 m (4 ft) between the bottom of the grave and the highest the water table rises (1.5 m, 5 ft, in sand). For many graves together the water table should be at least 2.5 m (8 ft) down. The manual says these distances may need to be increased by soil conditions, and that a geological and hydrological opinion should be sought before any new burial ground is opened. A belt of deep-rooted plants, at least 10 m wide, around a burial ground helps.
- **Livestock (Tennessee Department of Agriculture and USDA-NRCS guide sheet 316):** more than 300 ft from a wellhead (up-slope), more than 165 ft from property lines and public areas, more than 100 ft from streams, ditches and water bodies, and at least 2 ft above bedrock and the seasonal high water table, in soil that water moves through slowly. Large animals get at least 2 ft of soil cover. The guide sheet tells you to look at fractured or cavernous bedrock, but it does not mention sinkholes or limestone by name. Treat that as your warning, not your clearance. The human-remains manual also says its distances change with the local geology and water.
- Those figures are floors. In karst, in a high water table, in coarse soil, upslope of a well or in a floodplain, they are not enough. If your site meets the number and fails the soil, water or karst checks, it fails.
- Keep every grave and burial pit **down-slope** of wells and springs where possible, and shape the ground so surface water runs around it. Mound the soil, and plant grass at once.
- Never bury where you may need to dig a well, a cistern or a garden.
- After any burial, keep the water protection on: filter and treat all water, and watch for changes in taste, smell or colour, or sudden illness. If anything changes, stop using that source and treat it as contaminated.

## Protective equipment and hygiene
Handle remains as you would handle blood and body fluids: assume they may be infectious.
- The manual's minimum is waterproof gloves, an apron and boots. Wear gloves once only, and throw them away safely. Wear heavy gloves over them if there are sharp edges or bone, and eye or face protection against splashes. Cover your own cuts with waterproof dressings. Face masks are not essential for health; wear one if it helps you cope.
- Never wipe your face or mouth with your hands. Wash hands with soap and treated water after handling and before eating or drinking.
- Faecal leakage from a body is common. Keep the body bagged or wrapped, and clean any spill with soap and water. Wash tools, boards, wheelbarrows and clothes afterwards.
- Do not use lime or chemicals "to make it safe". The manual says lime does not hasten decomposition and disinfectant is not needed, and it advises against lime and other chemicals in graves. Soap and water on hands, clothes and gear is what works.
- After some days, closed and unventilated spaces (a cellar, a shed, a vehicle) can hold harmful gases from decomposition. Open them and let fresh air in before you go in.
- Do not take on remains where there is a chemical or radiation risk, or where people died of a highly infectious illness, unless you are trained.
- **A body that may have died of something highly infectious** (cholera, a fever with bleeding such as Ebola, a fast-spreading illness)? The manual says untrained people should not manage these deaths. If you have no choice, use much more protection (double gloves, gown, apron, boots, face shield), keep the number of helpers to the fewest, keep children and visitors away, limit any gathering, and wash all clothes and gear. Hand this work to trained people if any exist.
- Look after the people doing the work. Take breaks. Talk afterwards. This is hard and it stays with people.

## Records: write it down every time
A written record protects your water, the family's wishes, the law and the future owners of the land. For each death or burial, write:
- Date, time and place of death or finding; who found it; who identified the person.
- Name, age, description; a unique code for each person if there is more than one, on a waterproof label tied to the body or bag (writing on the bag alone can rub off). Photos of the face (if present) and any ID or marks.
- What was done: how it was kept, what it was wrapped in, where it was buried, how deep, and how it was marked.
- The site: a sketch with measurements to two fixed landmarks, the compass bearing, GPS if you have it, and the **Site Check sheet** (soil, depth to water and rock, slope, karst signs, distance to wells, springs and water, rules checked).
- Who was told (family, authorities), and the person's belongings.
- For animals: species, number, cause if known, date, method and place.
Keep the records dry, in at least two places. Mark each grave with a lasting marker (stone or concrete, not wood or anything that rots or can be moved), write the code on it, and record GPS or measured positions. File a map of any family burial ground with the property deed, so it is protected and findable later.

## Human remains: the steps
Treat the dead with dignity and care. The family's wishes, faith and customs matter, and so does the law.
- **1. Make sure of the death.** If phones work and the death was sudden, unexpected, violent or unattended, call 911. If someone may only be unconscious, treat them as alive (see the CPR card).
- **2. Record and identify first.** Photograph the face, note marks, take ID and personal items. Do this early: it matters most in the first day or two.
- **3. Cover and keep cool.** Wrap in a clean sheet or bag, label it, and keep it somewhere as cold as you can make it, shaded and apart from food and water. The manual's best storage is refrigeration at 2 to 4 °C (35.6 to 39.2 °F); failing that, a cold protected place out of direct sun, or temporary burial. In heat, bodies change fast; in winter, an unheated shed works.
- **4. Tell the family and your group.** Allow a farewell and the customs the person would have wanted. Keep gatherings small if the death may have been infectious.
- **5. Choose the arrangement.** Burial at your recorded group burial ground, a crematory if one is working, or a funeral home or the county, while services still run. Check the law while there is a law to check, and record who decided and why. Without outside authority, the family and the group leader decide together, and write it down.
- **6. If you bury:** use the site only after the paper, karst and field checks pass. Dig 1.5 to 3 m (5 to 10 ft) deep, with 0.9 to 1.2 m (3 to 4 ft) of soil on top, and keep the bottom well above the highest groundwater. That is a lot of digging by hand: share the work and rest. If you cannot dig that deep above the water, that is a reason not to bury here, not a reason to dig shallower. Use a shroud or a plain wood coffin that breaks down quickly, and avoid biodegradable body bags and chemicals (the manual advises against them). Mark the grave. Draw the map and record it.
- **7. Afterwards:** wash up, record, treat water as usual, and check on the people who did the work.
- **Tennessee notes (check current law):** no state law forbids burying on your own land, but your county or town may have zoning and health rules, and a death certificate or medical examiner report may be needed. A family burial ground recorded with the deed is protected under state law. All human remains are protected: digging up a grave without authority is a serious crime. Cremation needs a permit.

## Livestock: the steps
Dispose of dead farm animals fast, within 48 hours. The Tennessee Department of Agriculture recommends it, whatever the weather.
- **1. Assess.** Count how many animals, what kind, about what weight each, and why they died. Note what you have: a landfill or renderer within reach, and clear, well-drained space for composting or burial. USDA's emergency guidance says the goal is to dispose of carcasses as soon as possible while containing disease, protecting the environment, and doing it at a cost you can bear, and that there is rarely one simple answer: you may need more than one method.
- **1b. Find out why it died.** If several animals die together, or it looks like a disease, keep people and other animals away, wear protection, and call the vet or the State Veterinarian if you can. Do not butcher or eat a sick animal.
- **2. Pick the method** in this order if you can: rendering or pick-up, a permitted landfill, **composting**, then burial. Composting above ground is often the safest choice where the soil or the water is a worry.
- **3. Compost (USDA-NRCS standard 317 and the Tennessee guide sheet):** build on a firm, flat, well-drained pad, away from wells and water, under a roof or a sheet of 6-mil plastic. Start with a foot of carbon (sawdust, wood chips, straw or similar), put the animal on it, cover all sides with at least a foot of the same, keep it damp like a wrung-out sponge, and watch the temperature: it should reach 130 to 150 °F. Turn it when it peaks. Cover bones and parts that stick out.
- **4. Bury only if the site passes every check**, and only large animals one layer thick. Cover with at least 2 ft of soil, more or add a fence if coyotes, dogs and others dig. Mound it to shed water and plant it.
- **5. Do not** dump carcasses in a sinkhole, a creek, a pond, a gully or a woodlot where the water runs off.
- **6. Large losses:** more than 10,000 pounds, tell the State Veterinarian. Landfill, composting or rendering may be the only practical ways.
- **7. Record** species, number, date, method and place.

## Pets: the steps
A pet's death is a loss. Be kind to the family, and keep the water safe.
- **1. Wear gloves,** wrap the pet in a towel or sheet and a bag, and keep it cool.
- **2. If a vet put the pet down,** the drug can poison any animal that digs it up and eats it. Bury it deep, keep it fenced, or use another method.
- **3. Choose a method:** a vet, a pet cremation service, a pet cemetery, a local pick-up, or burial on your own land at a site that passes every check. Check your county's rules: they vary, and the Tennessee farm-animal rules cover livestock, not pets.
- **4. If you bury:** pick high, dry, deep soil, well away from wells, springs, creeks and any sinkhole. Dig deep enough that dogs and coyotes cannot reach it (more than 2 ft of cover). Keep it away from the garden and the place where you will build.
- **5. Mark and record** the place. Wash your hands with soap and water.
- **6. If the pet died of something catching** (or you do not know), do not touch it bare-handed and keep other pets away. Treat it as an infected animal: bag it, double wrap, and use a vet, cremation or deep burial at a verified site.

## Wildlife and game: the steps
Wild animals found dead, and the leavings from hunting, are different from your own animals.
- **1. Do not touch a wild animal that is dead or acting oddly** with bare hands. Animals can carry rabies, tularemia, avian flu and other diseases. Use gloves and a shovel, wash after, and keep dogs away.
- **2. A small number of dead wild animals** on open land usually can be left alone. Leave them well away from water, paths and people. Do move one that is in your well area, near the house, or in a water source.
- **3. Many dead birds or animals at once** can be a sign of disease or poison. Do not handle them. Keep pets and livestock away, note the date and place, and tell the wildlife agency or the vet if you can.
- **4. Game offal, hides and bones** from your own hunting: keep them away from water and campsites. Bury them well away from water, at least 2 ft deep, or compost them with carbon, or take them well away from the camp, where scavengers will clear them. Never dump them in a stream, a sinkhole or beside a well.
- **5. Deer in an area with chronic wasting disease:** check the current state wildlife agency rules before moving a carcass or its parts.
- **6. Record** large events: date, place, species, number.

## If you cannot bury: alternatives
Burial is not always possible or wise: karst ground, a high water table, frozen or rocky ground, flooding, or no safe site.
- **Cold storage:** keep remains wrapped and cold, apart from food and water, until a safe method is possible. This works for humans, pets, and small animals for a limited time. In hot weather it does not last. In winter, a cold outbuilding, or a cold cellar that is not part of your water system, can hold remains for days.
- **Composting above ground (animals, not humans):** a covered pile with carbon material is the safest answer for livestock where soil or water is a worry.
- **Rendering, landfill, crematory, or the county,** while they are working: they are built for this. Call first. Do not count on them: after a few months they may be gone, so choose your own site and method early.
- **Compost animals above ground,** on a pad you built, with carbon material you stored (sawdust, wood chips, straw, leaves). This needs no machinery and no outside help.
- **A group burial ground** on the best site you have found and recorded. Use it for all human remains, in order, and keep the map.
- **Burning (animals):** a last resort. It is smoky, hard to finish, can start fires, and may need a permit or be against the law where you live. Dispose of the ash like other waste.
- **Move it and bury it elsewhere:** a better site on higher, deeper ground, further from water. Do not move human remains far or in the open; wrap and keep them cold while you arrange it.
- **Ask** the county health department, the vet, Extension or a funeral director, while any can be reached. When none can, rely on the checks above and the group's records.

## Check the work: every season
every: 90
Walk each burial and compost site. Look for sinking ground, seeping liquid, bad smell, scavenger digging, bare soil and standing water. Fix the cover, the fence and the drainage. Test or taste-check nothing: **treat all water**, and look for signs your water changed. Update the map and records.
